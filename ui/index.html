      Calgary’s potential as a tourist hub

      Published November 10, 2026 · 3 min 50 s · animated, narrated, with music

      Calgary welcomed 10.5 million visitors in 2025, who spent $3.3 billion; hotels were 67.5% full and tourism and hospitality supported 84,000 jobs. About two dollars in three came from Canadians (Alberta $1.0 billion, the rest of Canada $1.2 billion) and one in three from abroad (United States $538 million, overseas $521 million), and international visitor spending rose 15.6% in the first quarter of 2026. YYC handled 19.4 million passengers with 110 non-stop destinations, and more than 7 million international travellers connect through it each year without leaving the airport: every 1% who stayed would be 70,000 more visitors. Banff National Park had a record 4.5 million visitors in 2025–26, and five UNESCO World Heritage Sites are within a few hours’ drive. The goal is $6 billion in visitor spending by 2035, about 6% growth a year.

      Transcript

      Opening. Calgary. A city of big skies, with the Rocky Mountains on its doorstep. How far could tourism here really go? Here is what the numbers say.

      Today. In twenty twenty-five, Calgary welcomed ten and a half million visitors. They spent three point three billion dollars. Hotels were sixty-seven and a half percent full. And tourism and hospitality supported eighty-four thousand jobs.

      Who spends. So where does that money come from? One billion dollars comes from Albertans, and one point two billion from the rest of Canada. Visitors from the United States and overseas add just over one billion more. Put together, about two dollars in every three come from Canadians, and one in three from abroad. And that international share is growing. In the first quarter of twenty twenty-six, international visitor spending rose more than fifteen percent.

      The airport. The hub is already built. Calgary's airport handled nineteen point four million passengers in twenty twenty-five. It flies non-stop to one hundred and ten destinations. Fifteen new routes were added in a single year. And twenty twenty-six brought its first non-stop flights to South America and the Middle East: Sao Paulo, and Abu Dhabi.

      The opportunity. Here is the opportunity. More than seven million international travellers connect through Calgary every year, and never leave the airport. What if a few of them stayed? Every one percent who stay would mean seventy thousand more visitors. It has already started. Since June twenty twenty-six, a stopover program lets travellers on two WestJet routes from the United Kingdom add up to seven days in Calgary, at no extra airfare.

      The gateway. Calgary is also a base camp. Banff National Park just set a record: four and a half million visitors in a single year. And Calgary is its nearest international airport, about one hundred and thirty kilometres away. Five UNESCO World Heritage Sites sit within a few hours' drive, along with the Badlands, dinosaur country, and Blackfoot heritage.

      Events. And the city already knows how to draw a crowd. The Calgary Stampede generated seven hundred and twenty-one million dollars across Alberta in twenty twenty-five, a third more than in twenty nineteen. Meetings and conventions brought seventy-five thousand delegates, and two hundred and forty million dollars. Sport, culture and major events added nearly one hundred and twenty-three million more.

      Lessons from elsewhere. Other places have turned passing traffic into tourism. Iceland offered a free stopover of up to seven days. Its visitor numbers roughly tripled in six years. Singapore gives transit passengers a free city tour. More than eighty thousand took it in twenty nineteen. Denver, another gateway to the Rockies, measured two hundred and nineteen dollars in visitor spending for every dollar of advertising. And Dubai, with festivals all year round, fills almost eighty-one percent of its hotel rooms. Calgary fills sixty-seven and a half. Not everything will carry over to Calgary. But the ideas have been tested.

      The target. Tourism Calgary has set a target: double visitor spending, to six billion dollars, by twenty thirty-five. That means growing about six percent, every year, for ten years. Growth has costs too: crowding in Banff, and pressure on housing from short-term rentals. They need weighing alongside the gains.

      The city above the city: Calgary’s Plus 15

      Published November 9, 2026 · 1 min 47 s · animated, narrated, with music

      In 1969, one enclosed bridge crossed 4 Avenue SW, linking the Calgary Inn (today’s Westin) to Calgary Place, about 15 feet above the street. Today the City counts 86 Plus 15 bridges and more than 16 km of walkways. The City’s records show 11 bridges built in the 1970s and 31 in the 1980s. On the busiest bridges, 19,000 to 22,000 people crossed every winter workday in City counts from 2011 and 2012, and January counts were about double July’s. The City’s own study found uneven hours, the name signed 11 different ways, and some routes with stairs but no ramp.

      Transcript

      The mystery. Imagine walking through downtown Calgary in the middle of winter. Snow is falling. The streets are cold. But what if you could cross most of downtown without stepping outside? Welcome to the city above the city.

      The original vision. In the nineteen sixties, Harold Hanen, an architect in the City's planning department, imagined downtown buildings joined by enclosed bridges, about fifteen feet above the street. In nineteen sixty-nine, the first one was built, linking the Calgary Inn, today's Westin, to Calgary Place.

      An entire network. One bridge became many. The City's records show eleven more in the nineteen seventies, and thirty-one in the boom of the nineteen eighties. Today, the City counts eighty-six bridges and more than sixteen kilometres of walkways, linking offices, hotels, shops and transit.

      The weather effect. And the weather shapes how people use it. In City counts from twenty eleven and twenty twelve, the busiest bridges carried nineteen to twenty-two thousand people every winter workday. The City's network study found January counts were about double July's.

      The challenges. But a city above the city has its challenges. Most walkways run through private buildings, and owners keep different hours. Standard hours end at nine on weeknights and seven on weekends. A City study found the network's name signed eleven different ways, and some routes with stairs but no ramp.

      The bigger question. More than fifty years after the first bridge, the Plus Fifteen is still one of Calgary's boldest experiments. And it raises a bigger question for winter cities: should people move through the city above its streets, or on them? Explore Calgary's stories at calgary insight dot c a.

Pedestrian counts are historical (2011 and 2012). Moving people and dimmed bridges are illustrations, not counts. The 1969 drawing is a newspaper illustration of the time. Sources: City of Calgary, Plus 15 network (86 bridges, 16+ km, hours); City of Calgary, Plus 15 Policy (2021: first bridge 1969, private property and easements); City of Calgary, Plus 15 Network Study (2019: pedestrian counts, January and July, signage and accessibility); City of Calgary open data, Plus 15 Bridges (fu7z-c7ar), Plus 15 walkways (3u3x-hrc7) and 3D Buildings – Citywide (cchr-krqg); Heritage Calgary (Harold Hanen and the 1966 plan); map data © OpenStreetMap contributors.       The pipes beneath Calgary: how old are our sewer connections?

      Published November 8, 2026 · 3 min 34 s · animated, narrated, with music

      The City’s open data doesn’t include its sewer mains, so this looks at the connections: the City-owned section of each home’s sewer line, from the main to the property line (367,671 with an install year; 2,782 km). The typical connection went in in 1995; 24.8% are older than 50. Against published service lives for each material, 32,720 (8.9%) are past their typical life: 25.8% on the short benchmark, 2.3% on the long one. If none were replaced, that would reach 43,464 by 2046 and 77,608 by 2071. Four in five of those past typical life have no specific material recorded (“Other” 17,877, code “TP” 7,582, blank 957) and are given 45 years. Age is a warning sign, not proof that a pipe has failed.

      Transcript

      Opening. Right now, beneath Calgary's streets, nearly thirty-three thousand of the pipes that carry sewage away from homes and buildings have passed the life that pipes like them are expected to last.

      The wave ahead. And if none were replaced, that number would more than double by twenty seventy-one. So, how old are the pipes under your feet?

      What is counted. The City's open data doesn't include its sewer mains, so this study looks at the connections. The City publishes the public section of each home's connection, from the main to the property line. Nearly three hundred sixty-eight thousand of them, about two thousand eight hundred kilometres of pipe.

      The method. Each record has the year the pipe went in, and what it's made of. We set each pipe's age against published service lives for its material: a short, a typical, and a long benchmark.

      How old. The typical connection went in in nineteen ninety-five, so it's thirty-one years old. But one in four is more than fifty.

      Materials. Two in three are plastic, expected to last about a century. One in five is clay tile, mostly from the sixties and seventies. Cast iron, expected to last about sixty years, is three percent. And half of it is already past that.

      The verdict. Against the typical life for each material, about nine percent of connections are past it. Judged by the short end of the range, it's one in four. By the long end, about two percent.

      One honest caveat. One honest caveat. Four in five of the pipes past their typical life have no specific material on record, so the study gives them a cautious forty-five years. Age is a warning sign, not proof that a pipe has failed.

      Where. The oldest networks are in Calgary's established communities. Among those with at least a thousand connections, Inglewood, Renfrew and Banff Trail are oldest, with the typical pipe from the mid nineteen-fifties. In Wildwood, three in four connections are past their typical life.

      The newest. In the newest communities, like Alpine Park and Glacier Ridge, the typical pipe is just two or three years old.

      The wave. The bigger story is the wave behind them. The boom years of the seventies laid more connections than any decade before. As those pipes age, the count past typical life climbs. About forty-three thousand by twenty forty-six, and seventy-eight thousand by twenty seventy-one.

      The fix: relining. The City is already fixing pipes from the inside, sliding a new liner through the old one, with little or no digging. Its records list about four thousand relined connections. Seven hundred ninety-one are dated twenty twenty-five, more than three times as many as in twenty seventeen.

      311 requests. And residents report sewer problems. In twenty twenty-five, Calgarians made about fourteen thousand three-one-one requests about sewer back-ups, sewer maintenance and catch basins.

      The bigger picture. Calgary isn't alone. Across Canada in twenty twenty, nearly fourteen percent of smaller sewer pipes were rated poor or very poor. And a City report lists nine large wastewater river crossings, of unknown condition, among the assets whose failure would have severe consequences.

      What the data can’t show. What the open data can't show is the condition of each pipe. The City reports condition only as totals, in its asset management plan, not pipe by pipe. With pipe-by-pipe inspections, age could become a true picture of risk.

      The Elbow: Calgary’s First Peoples

      Published November 8, 2026 · 2 min 42 s · animated, narrated, with sound and music

      Before Calgary had a name, this place already had many: Mohkinstsis (Blackfoot), Wîchîspa Oyade (Stoney Nakoda), Guts’ists’i (Tsuut’ina) and Otos-kwunee (Métis), all meaning “the elbow”. About 8,000 years of people where the Bow and Elbow meet, the stone circles on Nose Hill, Treaty 7 in 1877, the hard years after the buffalo disappeared and the residential schools, and what the Nations are doing today: Elbow River Camp since 1912, the Buffalo Treaty and 130+ bison back in Banff, Kainai’s fire guardians, Áísínai’pi as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and Taza.

      Transcript

      Opening. Before Calgary had a name, this place already had many. In Blackfoot, Stoney, Tsuut’ina, and Métis, they all mean the same thing: the elbow.

      Calgary’s First Peoples. This is the story of Calgary's First Peoples.

      Eight thousand years. For at least eight thousand years, people have lived where the Bow and Elbow rivers meet.

      The Nations. The Blackfoot Confederacy: Siksika, Kainai, and Piikani, with the Blackfeet across the border in Montana. The Tsuut’ina, who speak a Dene language. The Stoney Nakoda, people of the mountains. And the Métis, who wintered along the Bow.

      The buffalo. Life followed the buffalo. Families moved with the seasons, used every part of the animal, and set careful fires to renew the grass that drew the herds.

      Nose Hill. Their homes left marks you can still see. On Nose Hill, stone circles that once held down lodge covers still rest in the grass.

      Treaty 7. In eighteen seventy-seven, the five Nations made Treaty Seven with the Crown. The Elders say it was a treaty of peace, and of sharing the land.

      Hard years. Then came hard years. By eighteen seventy-nine, the buffalo were nearly gone. For more than a century, children were sent to residential schools, ten of them in and around Calgary.

      They held on. But the Nations held on to their languages, their ceremonies, and their stories.

      Elbow River Camp. Since the first Stampede in nineteen twelve, Treaty Seven families have raised painted tipis by the Elbow River. Some designs are hundreds of years old.

      The buffalo come home. In twenty fourteen, Nations from both sides of the border signed the Buffalo Treaty, to bring the buffalo home. In twenty seventeen, sixteen bison returned to Banff. By twenty twenty-four, the herd had grown past one hundred and thirty.

      Fire guardians. The Kainai Nation is building Canada's first fire guardians program, bringing good fire back to heal the prairie.

      Áísínai’pi. In twenty nineteen, Writing-on-Stone, a sacred Blackfoot place of rock art, became a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

      Today. Today, more than forty-eight thousand Indigenous people live in the Calgary region. Next door, the Tsuut’ina Nation and its partner Canderel are building Taza, with shops, hotels, and entertainment along Tsuut’ina Trail.

      Close. Long before it was Calgary, it was the elbow. Their story is Calgary's story. calgaryinsight.ca.

      Where Two Rivers Meet: The Story of Calgary

      Published November 8, 2026 · 2 min 36 s · animated, narrated, with sound and music

      Calgary has burned, flooded, struck oil and hosted the world. From Mohkinstsis at the meeting of the Bow and Elbow rivers to a city of more than 1.5 million: the North-West Mounted Police fort (1875), the name Fort Calgary (1876), Treaty 7 (1877), the railway (1883), the 1886 fire and the Sandstone City, the first Stampede (1912), oil at Turner Valley (1914), the Leduc boom (1947), the Calgary Tower (1968), the 1988 Winter Olympics and the 2013 flood.

      Transcript

      Opening. Calgary has burned, flooded, struck oil, and hosted the world. It all began where two rivers meet.

      Mohkinstsis. For thousands of years, the place where the Bow and Elbow rivers join was a gathering place. The Blackfoot call it Mohkinstsis, the elbow.

      The Nations. It is the land of the Blackfoot Confederacy, the Tsuut’ina, and the Stoney Nakoda, and home to the Métis.

      1875. Eighteen seventy-five. The North-West Mounted Police arrive, and build a log fort where the rivers meet.

      The name. Its commander names it Fort Brisebois, after himself. Nobody likes it. In eighteen seventy-six, it becomes Fort Calgary, named by James Macleod after Calgary, on Scotland's Isle of Mull.

      1877. Eighteen seventy-seven. Treaty Seven is signed at Blackfoot Crossing, between the Crown and five First Nations.

      1883. Eighteen eighty-three. The Canadian Pacific Railway arrives. Calgary becomes a town the next year, and a city in eighteen ninety-four.

      1886. Eighteen eighty-six. Fire sweeps through the wooden downtown. Calgary rebuilds in stone, and becomes the Sandstone City.

      1912. Nineteen twelve. Guy Weadick stages the first Stampede, backed by four cattlemen known as the Big Four.

      1914. Nineteen fourteen. Dingman Number One strikes at Turner Valley, southwest of the city. Calgary goes oil crazy.

      1947. Nineteen forty-seven. After the big Leduc discovery, oil companies make Calgary their head office, and the city booms.

      1968. Nineteen sixty-eight. The Husky Tower rises one hundred and ninety-one metres over downtown. Today, we call it the Calgary Tower.

      1988. Nineteen eighty-eight. Calgary hosts the first Winter Olympics ever held in Canada, and the tower's torch burns for sixteen days.

      2013. Twenty thirteen. The Bow and Elbow rise. About seventy-five thousand people are told to leave home, the largest evacuation in the city's history. Calgarians clean up, together.

      Today. Today, more than one and a half million people call Calgary home. A city of newcomers, still growing, where two rivers meet.

      Budget 2026: what's in it for Alberta and Calgary

      Published November 6, 2026 · 2 min 47 s · animated, narrated, with sound

      The federal budget tabled on November 5, 2026, searched word by word: Alberta is named 34 times, Calgary twice and Edmonton five times (Québec 74, Ontario 67, British Columbia 41).
