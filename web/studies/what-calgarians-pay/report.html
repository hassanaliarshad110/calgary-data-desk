What Calgarians pay: the proposed budget, and Calgary against seven Canadian cities

City property tax and utility bills for a typical home, 2026 to 2030

Calgary · November 10, 2026 · Rates and budgets published to November 10, 2026

QuestionWhat would the proposed 2027 to 2030 budget cost a typical Calgary household compared with 2026, and do Calgarians pay more, less or the same as people in other large Canadian cities?

Key findings

  1. 1
    In 2026 a typical Calgary home pays $369 a month to the City: $229 in property tax and $140 for water, wastewater, stormwater and waste. Of eight large Canadian cities, that is the lowest; the other seven average $467, and Saskatoon ($378) and Winnipeg ($388) are close.
  2. 2
    Calgary's City property tax on a typical home ($2,752 a year) is the third lowest of the eight, and its City utility bill at the same water use ($140 a month) is also the third lowest. Measured against median household income (2021 Census, 2020 incomes), Calgarians pay the smallest share of the eight (4.5%), partly because Calgary incomes are among the highest. That share is a rough affordability guide, not the burden on homeowners: it sets a homeowner's bill against the income of all households, renters included.
  3. 3
    The proposed 2027 budget adds about $53 a month ($632 a year) for a typical home: $37 in property tax and $16 for water and waste. The 15% is the increase in the property tax the City collects from existing properties, not the change on every bill: on the City's typical home, whose assessment falls 2.5%, the tax rises about 16%, and other homes will see more or less. Using the 2027 figures other cities have published so far, Calgary would be the third lowest of the eight, about $57 a month below the others' average instead of $98. Toronto, Ottawa and Vancouver have not yet published 2027 figures, and Edmonton's and Regina's are still proposals, so this will change.
  4. 4
    A scenario, not a bill anyone has set: if the property tax increases reported for 2028 to 2030 pass, water bills rise as Council approved in October (to $206.94 a month by 2030) and cart fees rise as the City proposed in October (to $26.31), a typical home would pay about $567 a month in 2030, 54% more than in 2026 ($2,379 a year more, before inflation). The tax on a typical home would rise about 46% over four years; Edmonton's proposed path for its tax increases is about 19%.
  5. 5
    In short: today Calgarians pay less to their City than people in the other seven cities. The proposed budget would narrow that difference in 2027, and the full four-year path would narrow it much further; how Calgary compares by 2030 depends on decisions the other cities have not yet made.

1What this study asks, and how it answers

The City of Calgary released its proposed 2027 to 2030 budget on November 10, 2026. It asks for a 15% increase in property tax revenue from existing properties in 2027, alongside water rates Council approved in October. This study asks two plain questions: what would this cost a typical Calgary household compared with 2026, and do Calgarians pay more, less or the same as people in other large Canadian cities?

It compares eight cities: Calgary, Edmonton, Vancouver, Toronto, Ottawa, Winnipeg, Saskatoon and Regina. For each it takes the City property tax on the typical home the city itself reports on, and the City utility bill for a single-family home: water, wastewater, stormwater and waste collection, at the same water use everywhere (19 cubic metres a month, the City of Calgary's typical customer and the water use of its own Residential Property Taxes and Utility Charges Survey). These are the charges a council sets directly. Provincial school tax is left out, and so are electricity and natural gas, which councils do not set (although some, Calgary included, add franchise fees to those bills).

Every figure comes from the cities' own budgets, rate schedules and tax notices, or from the City of Winnipeg's published 2026 comparison of tax bills; where a city has only published a percentage, from news reports of its budget. Each is labelled, and figures derived from what a city published are marked.

One choice matters: this study compares what an ordinary homeowner in each city pays on that city's typical home. The City of Calgary's own survey instead prices one identical house in every city. On that basis its last edition (2023) put Calgary 7th of 14 cities for City tax on a bungalow and 9th of 14 for tax and utilities together, though lowest of 10 for a two-storey house. The two methods answer different questions and cover different sets of cities, so their rankings differ; both are worth reading.

2What the proposed budget would cost a typical Calgary home

The City's own estimate, for a home assessed at the median ($706,000 in 2026, $688,000 in 2027): property tax rises from $229 to $266 a month and the water, wastewater, stormwater and waste bill from $140 to $156, a combined $53 a month, or about $632 a year. The City's 2026 utility figure is $139.72: $119.21 for water, wastewater and stormwater and $20.51 for the black, blue and green carts. Its 2027 figure of $156 is rounded: $133.71 for water, wastewater and stormwater and $21.96 for the carts at the rate the City proposed in October 2026 ($155.67).

Why the typical home sees about 16%, not 15%. These are two different measures: the 15% is a citywide revenue figure, and about 16% is the City's estimate for its typical home. The 15% is the increase in the property tax the City collects from existing properties: about $384 million, as each 1% raises about $25.6 million. It is not the increase on every bill. A home's City tax is its assessed value times the tax rate, and Council sets the 2027 rate in spring 2027, once the value of all property is known. The rate is set so that existing property together pays 15% more; each home then pays according to its own assessment.

For its typical home the City gives $229.32 a month in 2026 (its estimate when the 2026 budget was approved) and about $266 in 2027: about 16% more (15.8% to 16.2%, as $266 is rounded to the dollar). That home's assessment falls 2.5%, from $706,000 to $688,000, so the City's figure implies a 2027 residential rate about 19% above 2026 (about 0.464% of value, against 0.389%): the rate rises more than 15% because the City expects assessments to fall (the typical home's by 2.5%). A home whose assessment falls less than the typical home's will see more than 16%, and one that falls more will see less. The City has not yet published the 2027 assessment roll or tax rates, so this study does not break the 16% down further; the calculator with this study, and the calculation workbook, show it for any home.

LiveWire Calgary, reporting on the budget, describes the 15% as the largest yearly increase since 1996, after increases that averaged 3.28% over the previous five years, the second lowest among large Canadian municipalities.

Water is the second part. In 2027 the typical water bill rises 12.2% (+$14.50), most of it wastewater (+15.9%); the budget includes upgrades to the Pine Creek and Fish Creek wastewater treatment plants and new feedermains to increase drinking water capacity. A homeowner's actual change depends on how their assessment moves against the median and how much water they use; the calculator with this study shows it for any home.

3The four years to 2030

The City's budget page gives the household figure for 2027 only. News reports of the budget presented to Council give the later property tax increases as 10% in 2028, 7.7% in 2029 and 6% in 2030, the water rates Council approved in October take the typical water bill from $119.21 to $206.94 a month by 2030 (nearly 74%), and the cart fees the City proposed in October rise $1.45 a year, from $20.51 to $26.31 a month. Applied to the typical home, this scenario gives:

  • property tax would rise from $229 a month in 2026 to about $334 in 2030 (+46%);
  • the water bill from $119 to about $207;
  • cart fees from $20.51 to $26.31;
  • together, from $369 to about $567 a month, $2,379 more a year (+54%).

These are dollars of each year, before inflation; if prices rise 2% a year, the Bank of Canada's target, they rise about 8% over the same four years. The later years are a scenario built on reported figures, not bills Council has set: Council can change each year and assessments shift. The 2030 water bill ($206.94) comes from the rates Council approved, as reported; the cart fees are the City's October 2026 proposal, which its $156 estimate for 2027 already reflects; the water bill in 2028 and 2029 is spread in equal steps between 2027 and 2030 because this study did not find the yearly figures.

4City property tax on a typical home, eight cities in 2026

Calgary's City property tax on its median home is $2,752 a year ($229 a month), the third lowest of the eight, close to Winnipeg ($2,581) and Saskatoon ($2,716). Regina is about $337 a year higher; Edmonton, Vancouver, Toronto and Ottawa are $1,061 to $1,841 a year higher. The City of Winnipeg's own 2026 comparison gives Calgary $2,741, within $11 of this study's figure.

A typical home is not the same house everywhere: Vancouver's median single-family home is assessed at about $2.1 million, Regina's average home at about $324,000. Toronto's average home includes condominiums; Toronto and Ottawa still assess homes at 2016 values. Saskatoon and Regina include their separate library levies, and Winnipeg its street renewal frontage levy, so that each figure covers what the other cities fund from City tax.

City tax also pays for different things in different provinces. Ontario cities fund some services that the province pays for in Alberta, and Vancouver homeowners pay TransLink separately for transit. For Vancouver this study counts the general purpose levy the City sets ($4,048); the same tax bill also carries TransLink, BC Assessment and Municipal Finance Authority levies, which some comparisons add in. Adding them would raise Vancouver's figure and would not change Calgary's position. Calgary's tax rate for business property is about 4.6 times its residential rate, which keeps the home share of City tax lower than it would otherwise be.

5City water, wastewater, stormwater and waste bills

At the same water use, Calgary's City utility bill is $140 a month, the third lowest of the eight; the others average $169. Toronto and Ottawa are lower, Saskatoon about $12 higher, and Winnipeg, Regina, Edmonton and Vancouver $33 to $72 higher.

Cities split these costs differently. Vancouver charges single-family homes flat fees that do not depend on use, and Metro Vancouver bills part of the water and sewer cost separately; Edmonton's waste service is paid entirely by its utility fee; Toronto's one water rate covers wastewater and stormwater too. Garbage cart sizes also differ (120 litres in Toronto, 240 in Edmonton, Saskatoon and Regina). That is why the next section adds tax and utilities together, which is the fairest single comparison.

Two figures are estimates: Edmonton's stormwater charge depends on lot size and runoff, and is estimated for a 6,000 square foot lot ($24.92 a month, close to the $24.62 Saskatoon's own comparison gave Edmonton for 2025); Saskatoon's storm water charge is its 2025 rate. Calgary's rates are per 30 days; the others are worked out for a 30.4-day month.

6Tax and utilities together, and against income

Together, a typical Calgary home pays the City $369 a month, the lowest of the eight, just below Saskatoon ($378) and Winnipeg ($388). Vancouver ($549), Edmonton ($521) and Ottawa ($513) are the highest. Of Calgary's $98 a month below the others' average, about $69 is property tax and the rest utilities.

Against what households earn, Calgary is also the lowest: tax and utilities take 4.5% of the median household's income, against 7.0% in Edmonton and 8.0% in Vancouver. This share is a rough guide only: Census incomes are for 2020, a pandemic year; they include renters, who do not pay property tax directly; and in Vancouver a single-family owner's bill is set against all households' income.

7Why tax rates alone mislead

Comparing tax rates on their own suggests Vancouver is the cheapest city and Regina among the dearest. A rate is set so that the city raises the money it needs from the total value of its property: where homes are worth more, the rate is lower. Calgary's 2026 City rate works out to about $3.90 for every $1,000 of a home's value, against $7.74 in Edmonton and $1.93 in Vancouver. What matters to a household is the dollars it pays, which is why this study compares dollars on a typical home.

A change in the tax rate also does not equal a change in a bill. Calgary's 15% is the increase in what the City collects; a home whose assessment falls less than the median will see more, and one that falls more will see less.

8Where other cities are heading in 2027

Other cities are setting their 2027 budgets at the same time. Edmonton proposes a 4.9% property tax increase (about $18 a month on its median home), Regina 5.81% with a 10.27% utility rate increase, Saskatoon approved 5.8% for 2027 a year ago, and Winnipeg's multi-year budget plans 3.5%, with its 2027 water and sewer rates approved. Toronto, Ottawa and Vancouver have not yet published their 2027 figures.

Putting the 2027 figures that exist beside the 2026 figures for the rest, Calgary's typical home would pay $422 a month, the third lowest of the eight and $57 below the others' average (against $98 in 2026). Because Toronto's, Ottawa's and Vancouver's 2027 figures are missing entirely, and Edmonton's and Saskatoon's 2027 utility rates too, those totals are understated, and Calgary's real position is likely to be somewhat better than shown.

Over four years the difference grows. The tax on a typical Calgary home would rise about 46% by 2030 on the reported path; Edmonton's proposed increases (4.9%, 4.7%, 4.5% and 3.9%) compound to about 19%. Comparing Calgary in 2030 with other cities needs their 2028 to 2030 decisions, which most have not made.

9What the money would buy

The budget is the City's plan for services and infrastructure, not only a bill. The City's highlights include $1.7 billion to upgrade the Pine Creek and Fish Creek wastewater treatment plants and $616 million for water feedermains, $420 million for pavement rehabilitation, $300 million for buses, $192 million for Calgary Police Service facilities and equipment, $102 million for new fire stations, $284 million for recreation facilities and $109 million for housing partners. The City says Calgary's growth, ageing infrastructure and rising costs drive the increase.

This study does not judge whether these investments are worth their cost; that is for Council and Calgarians to weigh. It sets out what the proposed budget would cost a household and how that compares, so that the debate can start from the same numbers. The full list of requests, with each business case, is on the City's budget page.

Conclusion

In 2026 a typical Calgary home pays its City $369 a month for property tax and utilities, the lowest of eight large Canadian cities and the smallest share of household income, though Saskatoon and Winnipeg are close. The proposed 2027 budget would add $53 a month and narrow Calgary's difference from the other cities, from about $98 to about $57 a month below their average on the figures published so far. If the later increases pass as reported, a typical home would pay about $567 a month by 2030, 54% more than in 2026, with water a large part of the rise. Where that leaves Calgary against other cities depends on their own decisions for 2028 to 2030.

What this cannot tell you

  • A typical home differs from city to city in value, age and size, and each city defines it differently (median or average; single-family homes or all homes, as in Toronto). The comparison is of what an ordinary homeowner pays, not the tax on one identical house; on an identical house the City's 2023 survey put Calgary in the middle of 14 cities.
  • Ottawa's 2026 tax is derived from the City's statement that its 3.75% increase adds $166 a year; it matches the City of Winnipeg's compiled $4,593. Saskatoon's and Regina's come from that compilation.
  • Edmonton's stormwater charge depends on lot area and a runoff factor; it is estimated for a 557 square metre lot with an assumed factor of 0.45. Saskatoon's storm water charge is its 2025 rate. Winnipeg's frontage levy uses the published $6.95 a foot and a 50-foot lot.
  • Calgary's 2028 to 2030 property tax increases are as reported in the news; the City's budget page gives 2027 only. Assessments, Council amendments and other cities' decisions will change every later year.
  • Census incomes are for 2020, a pandemic year, and include renters. They show relative burden between cities, not an exact share for homeowners today.
  • Electricity and natural gas are left out because councils do not set their prices, although councils add franchise fees to those bills (3% of Calgary's 2027 revenue) and Calgary's City-owned ENMAX pays the City a dividend (5%).
  • City tax pays for different things in different provinces (for example, transit in Vancouver is paid to TransLink, and Ontario cities fund some social services). The comparison is of what homeowners pay their city, not of value for money.
