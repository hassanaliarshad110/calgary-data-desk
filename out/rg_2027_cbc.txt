Regina's proposed 2027 budget includes 5.81% property tax bump, 10.27% utility increase

The City of Regina’s proposed budget for 2027 includes a property tax hike of 5.81 per cent and a utility rate increase of 10.27 per cent. 
The jump in property taxes would cost an extra $162.72 a year for the average property owner, defined as someone with a home valued at $324,000. The jump in utilities would tack on $235.68 for the same household. 
Budget deliberations are scheduled to begin on Dec. 7. 
"For people out there who are saying cut taxes, hold the line at zero, I'm basically trying to say don't hold your breath, it's not going to happen," acting city manager Jim Nicol said as he delivered the proposed budget Thursday morning. 
"It would result in serious reductions across the piece, and up until now that's not what we're hearing from residents, and that's not what council appears to be focusing on." 
A statement from the city said the largest increases to the operational budget are concentrated in "essential services," including a $15.6 million, or 28 per cent, increase in corporate level transactions. 
The proposal also includes more than $263 million in capital spending for 2027, including: 
Indoor aquatics facility: $122.0 million. 
Street infrastructure renewal: $9.9 million. 
Accessible sidewalk and concrete renewal: $9.5 million. 
Residential road renewal: $8.3 million. 
Civic fleet replacement: $8.3 million. 
Fire fleet replacement: $7.5 million. 
Transit fleet replacement: $7.2 million. 
Recreation facilities renewal: $5.9 million. 
Regina Police Service (RPS): $5.1 million. 
The proposed budget includes $11 million in total transit spending, as part of $115.7 million in planned spending over five years to introduce new buses and replace old ones. 
RPS has requested a net operating budget of almost $125 million, an increase of about $8.7 million over 2026. The city said that translates to a 2.3 per cent increase to the mill rate. 
Nicol said the proposed budget would continue to deliver the services and programs residents and council want. 
"They have to understand that for the city to continue to grow and develop and evolve, it costs money to have nice things," he said.
